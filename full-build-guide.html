FULLBUILD

Split-flap display — full build, beginner guide

Everything from ordering circuit boards to a finished wall display: shopping, 3D printing, a little soldering, flashing chips, snapping modules together, and wiring the whole thing to a Raspberry Pi. Every term is explained as it appears. The default display is 3 rows of 15 modules, but every step here works the same whether you build 5 modules or 45 — start small and grow.

01 How the display fits together

Each module is a self-contained letter: a 3D-printed drum holding 64 flaps, a small motor that turns it, and a little circuit board (the driver board) that controls the motor. The modules clip onto a metal mounting rail, and their backs press against shared bus boards that carry power and data past every module.

A Raspberry Pi runs a small web app. It speaks to the modules over RS-485 — two data wires shared by everyone. Each message names a module number, so all modules hear everything but only the addressed one acts. A 12 V power supply feeds the motors through the same bus boards.

RS-485 is a hardier version of a serial cable — two data wires, called A and B, designed to run long distances past electrically noisy things like 45 stepper motors. One pair of wires serves the entire display.

02 Plan the build order

total elapsed time ≈ 4–8 weeks, mostly waiting and printing

Two things dominate the calendar: circuit boards shipping from the factory (1–3 weeks) and printing 64 flaps per module. Order first, print while you wait, and the slow parts overlap:

  1. Order the PCBs (step 04) — longest lead time, so it goes first.
  2. Order everything else — motors, screws, magnets, pogo pins, power supply (step 03).
  3. 3D print while you wait (step 05) — flaps first, they take the longest.
  4. Solder + flash each driver board when the PCBs arrive (steps 06–07).
  5. Assemble one module completely and test it (steps 08–09) before mass-producing the rest.
  6. Mount, wire, and power the display (step 10).
  7. Set up the web app and calibrate (steps 11–12).

Start with fewer than 45. The protocol doesn't care how many modules exist. A single row of 5–15 modules is a complete, working display and a much friendlier first production run — you can add rows later with nothing more than more of the same.

04 Order the PCBs from JLCPCB

≈ 30 minutes at the keyboard · 1–3 weeks shipping · do this first

PCB = printed circuit board. JLCPCB is a factory that makes them cheaply from design files in this repo. Their PCBA (assembly) service also solders the tiny surface-mount components on for you — so the fiddly work arrives done, and only a few easy through-hole parts are left for your iron.

4a — Driver boards (with assembly)

Everything JLCPCB needs is in PCBs/driver-v4/FAB/ — use v4; it's the current revision and older ones are deprecated.

  1. Go to jlcpcb.com → Order Now and upload FAB.zip (the Gerber file — the board's blueprint).
  2. Set the quantity: one per module plus a few spares. The setup cost dominates, so extra boards are nearly free — and a spare will save you a three-week wait someday.
  3. Leave the board settings at their defaults. Pick any colour you like.
  4. Toggle PCB Assembly on → Standard PCBA → assembly side Top → Confirm.
  5. Upload the parts list BOM File.xls and the placement file CPL File.xlsx from the same folder.
  6. The site auto-matches each component to its catalogue number (they should match the C-numbers in BOM.md). Review anything it flags, check the totals, and order.

4b — Bus boards (bare boards, no assembly)

  • 5-module bus board (recommended — fewer boards to chain): upload PCBs/bus-board-5-module/FAB/5-module-bus-bar.zip as the Gerber. Order enough that modules ÷ 5, per row, are covered.
  • Single-module bus board: PCBs/bus-board/FAB/ has loose files rather than a zip — select everything in that folder, zip it yourself, and upload that.
  • No assembly toggle needed for either — they're just bare boards with contact pads.

05 3D print everything

days of printer time · start the flaps immediately

All the print files are in CAD Files/. The .3mf files open directly in Bambu Studio, PrusaSlicer, or OrcaSlicer with sensible settings already embedded — just open, slice, print.

5a — Flaps first: 64 per module

The flaps are the bulk of the printing — 64 per module is 2,880 for the full display — so start them the day you order the PCBs. CAD Files/64FlapsWithLetters (parts and bambu print profile)/ contains every flap as its own file plus 64FlapsPrintProfile.3mf, a ready-made Bambu profile with the colours already assigned.

  • Print 7 copies of the blank flap per module — its filename (Flap57 - Blank (print 7)) reminds you.
  • The set is: space, A–Z, 0–9, punctuation, and 8 solid-colour flaps (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, white). The coloured ones need matching filament — on a single-colour printer, batch the prints by colour.
  • flaps-generator/Flaps_holding_case.stl is an optional case for keeping printed flap sets sorted — with thousands of flaps in play, worth the print time.
  • Want your own font or characters? flaps-generator/ has an OpenSCAD generator with its own README.

06 Solder the through-hole parts

≈ 10 minutes per board · easy, repetitive soldering

The boards arrive with the hard parts done. Per board, you add four easy things — each is "push the part through its holes, solder the pins on the back, trim":

  1. The 5-pin JST-XH connector the motor plugs into (the notched side faces as marked on the board).
  2. The 3-pin connector for the Hall sensor lead.
  3. A 3-pin female header in the holes marked UPDI Interface — this is the programming socket used in step 07.
  4. The 4 pogo pins, in the column of holes marked 12V, B, A, G — springy tip pointing out the back of the board, so it can press onto the bus board.
Pogo pin — a tiny spring-loaded metal plunger. Four of them on each module press against four pads on the bus board, carrying power (12V, G) and data (A, B) with no wires or plugs. That's what makes modules click-in, click-out replaceable.

Solder pogo pins quickly. The spring inside is delicate — linger with the iron and you can cook it. Tack each pin in a second or two, let it cool, then finish the joint. Afterwards, press each tip: it should spring back freely.

07 Flash every board with its own address

≈ 30 minutes one-time setup, then ≈ 2 minutes per board

Flashing means copying the program (firmware) onto each board's chip, an ATtiny1616. The chip has no USB socket, so the UPDI Friend bridges: USB-C on the computer side, three wires into the header you soldered in step 06. Each chip keeps its program and its address through power-off, forever.

7a — One-time Arduino setup

  1. Install the Arduino IDE (free).
  2. Open Settings → Additional boards manager URLs and paste: 
    http://drazzy.com/package_drazzy.com_index.json
  3. Open Tools → Board → Boards Manager, search megaTinyCore, install it.
  4. Under Tools, set:
    • Board → megaTinyCore → ATtiny3216/1616/1606/816/806/416/406
    • Chip → ATtiny1616
    • Programmer → SerialUPDI — 230400 baud
    • Save EEPROM → retained
    • Leave everything else at defaults.
  5. Open firmware/splitflapfirmwarev7/splitflapfirmwarev7.ino (v7 is current).

7c — Flash each board

For each board in turn:

  1. In the sketch, near line 40, set this board's number: 
    const uint8_t HARDCODED_ID = 0;   // ← change per board: 0, 1, 2, …
  2. Plug the UPDI Friend's cable into the board's 3-pin header, matching labels: red → +3V3, black → G, white → UPDI. Set the Friend's voltage switch to 3V — it powers the chip while you flash, so no other power is connected.
  3. Plug the Friend into the computer via USB-C, pick the port under Tools → Port (it looks like /dev/cu.usbserial-…).
  4. Choose Sketch → Upload Using Programmer (⌘⇧U — not the normal Upload button) and wait for "Done uploading".
  5. Immediately write the number on the board with a marker or sticker. Forty-five identical green boards are indistinguishable ten minutes later.

Flashed two boards with the same number? No need to re-flash: connect the duplicate to the bus alone and send it the re-address command m*i<newId> from the test tool (step 09) — the new number sticks permanently. The chip stores its address in EEPROM, memory that survives power-off.

Doing all 45? The repo's programming-jig/ folder has a 3D-printable jig that presses onto a bare board's pads — no per-board soldering or clipping — plus its own README.

08 Assemble a module

≈ 30–45 minutes for your first, much faster after · the build video is a great visual companion

Do this once, carefully, and test it (step 09) before setting up a production line for the rest. Keep the build video handy for part orientation — this list is the order and the gotchas.

8a — Magnet and Hall sensor

  1. Super-glue the 3 × 1 mm magnet into its recess in the drum body.
  2. Fit the Hall sensor (on its 3-pin lead) into its mount in the enclosure, so the flat, printed face of the sensor faces the drum and sits close to the magnet's path.

8b — Motor and gears

  1. Press the motor gear onto the motor's shaft.
  2. Mount the motor to the enclosure with the M3 × 8 mm screws.
  3. Fit the center gear and the gear plate over it. Spin the gears by hand — they should turn smoothly with no grinding.

8c — Load the flaps

With the drum at its home position (magnet at the sensor), load all 64 flaps into the drum slots in exactly this order, starting with a blank flap at position zero: 

 ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ0123456789!@#$&()-+=;q:%'.,/?*roygbpw

That string starts with a space (the blank flap); q near the end stands for the double-quote flap, and the final roygbpw are the solid colours — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, white. Then fit the drum cap (lip) to lock them in.

Take your time here. Flap order is the one assembly mistake that can't be fixed in software. If every character later shows up exactly N flaps wrong, the stack went in shifted — you'll be opening the drum again.

8d — Close it up

  1. Mount the drum in the enclosure so it meshes with the gear train.
  2. Fit the driver board into its slot at the back, pogo pins facing out.
  3. Plug in the motor (5-pin JST) and the Hall sensor lead (3-pin), and route the wires under the wire retainer so they can't reach the drum.
  4. Fit the top and bottom covers with the M3 screws and nuts, and clip on the DIN rail mount.

09 Bench-test before mass production

≈ 15 minutes for the first module — do not skip

Test your first module on the desk before building 44 more, and give every later module a quick spin before it goes on the wall. Power the module by touching or clipping 12 V, G, A and B onto its pogo pins — a spare bus board with four wires soldered to it makes a perfect test cradle. (If you built the proof of concept, this is exactly your PoC wiring: Pi → converter → A/B, supply → 12V/G.)

  1. Power on. After a delay of its address × 150 ms, the module homes itself: the drum spins until the magnet passes the sensor, then advances to the blank flap and stops.
  2. Send it a letter. From the Pi (or any computer with an RS-485 connection), run the interactive test tool: 
    python3 -m pip install pyserial
python3 firmware/splitflapfirmwarev7/splitflap_test.py \
    --port /dev/serial0 --baud 9600
    Pick Show character → A, entering this module's address. The drum should flip round and frame a crisp letter A.
  3. Calibrate (menu option c) — the module measures its own drum rotation and stores the correction permanently. Once per module, on the bench, is the ideal time.

If anything misbehaves, the troubleshooting table in step 13 covers every common symptom. Fixing it now, on the desk, is ten times easier than fixing it on the wall.

10 Mount and wire the display

≈ 2–3 hours · the most satisfying step

10a — Rails, bus boards, modules

DIN rail — the standard ridged metal rail that electrical equipment clips onto. One horizontal rail per row of modules, screwed to your wall, frame, or backboard, spaced so the rows sit flush.
  1. Mount the DIN rails — one per row, level, spaced to the module height.
  2. Fix the bus boards behind the rail so that each module's four pogo pins land on a set of pads. Clip a module on temporarily to check alignment before fixing all of them.
  3. Chain the bus boards: wire each board's 12V, G, A and B to the next, all the way along each row, then link the rows. Same four lines everywhere — keep the wire colours consistent (red 12V, black G, blue A, green B) and this stays foolproof.
  4. Clip the modules on in address order — module 0 top-left, matching the map in step 07.

10b — Power

Mains electricity — the one genuinely dangerous thing in this project. The 12 V side is safe to touch; the 230/120 V mains side is not. If you use an open-frame or enclosure-mounted supply with a C14 inlet and switch, every mains connection must be insulated, strain-relieved, and enclosed before the plug goes anywhere near a socket. If any of that sounds unfamiliar, simply use a sealed 12 V desktop "brick" supply (like a beefy laptop charger) of 10 A or more — no exposed mains wiring, no risk, same result.

  1. Connect the supply's 12 V output to the bus: + to the 12V line, − to G. Double-check polarity before powering — reversed power is the one mistake that kills driver boards.
  2. Power the Pi from its own 5 V supply.

10c — Connect the Raspberry Pi

  1. Four female-female jumpers, Pi header → converter: pin 1 (3V3) → VCC, pin 6 (GND) → GND, pin 8 (TXD) → RXD, pin 10 (RXD) → TXD.
  2. Converter screw terminals to the bus: A+ → blue A line, B− → green B line. (Backwards is harmless — the display just stays silent until you swap them.)
  3. One-time, hand the Pi's serial pins over from the login console to your display: 
    sudo raspi-config
# Interface Options → Serial Port →
#   "login shell over serial?"          → No
#   "serial port hardware enabled?"     → Yes
sudo reboot
    Afterwards the port exists as /dev/serial0.

10d — First power-on

  1. Final glance: supply polarity, every module clipped fully home.
  2. Power up. Each module waits its address × 150 ms before homing, so the display wakes as a ripple running left-to-right, top-to-bottom — deliberate, so 45 motors never start at once. Total: about 7 seconds of the best sound in DIY.
  3. Any module that doesn't home: note its position, and see step 13.

11 The web app

≈ 20 minutes

The frontend is a Flask web app with a playlist system and ~20 built-in "apps" — clock, weather, stocks, countdowns, animations. On the Pi: 

python3 -m pip install flask pyserial pytz yfinance requests

Three small edits at the top of frontend/app.py before first run:

  • Line ~14 — set the serial port to /dev/serial0. (Baud stays 9600.)
  • Line ~16 — the settings path is hardcoded to /home/gordo/…; change gordo to your Pi username and run mkdir -p ~/splitflap.
  • Bottom of the file — change port 80 to 5000 to run without sudo. (And if your display isn't 3 × 15, the layout near the top is hardcoded too — adjust rows/columns to match.)
cd frontend
python3 app.py
# then browse to  http://<pi-address>:5000

Useful controls once you're in: Home All re-homes every module, Sync All pulls each module's stored calibration, and the playlist editor cycles between apps. To start on boot, add a systemd service or a @reboot cron entry running the same command.

12 Calibrate

≈ 2 minutes per module, once · already done for bench-tested modules

Every correction below is stored in the module's own EEPROM, so this is a one-time job — no reflashing, and it survives power-off. Use the test tool (step 09) with the whole display connected; each command targets one module by address.

  • Calibrate (c) — the module spins the drum, measures its true steps-per-revolution, and stores it. Run once per module if you didn't on the bench.
  • Nudge (s) — if a module's characters all sit slightly high or low in the window, nudge a few steps at a time until "A" is perfectly framed. The correction is folded into its stored home offset.
  • If a module's characters are off by whole flaps (ask for A, always get C), that's the flap-loading mistake from step 8c — the drum needs reloading with the blank at home.

13 Troubleshooting

SymptomMost likely fixes, in order
Nothing responds anywhere on the bus Swap the A and B wires at the converter (the classic RS-485 swap — completely safe). Check the TX/RX jumpers cross over (Pi TXD → converter RXD). Confirm the serial console was disabled in raspi-config and the port is /dev/serial0 at 9600.
One module never responds Duplicate or wrong address — connect it to the bus alone and re-address with m*i<newId>. Also check its pogo pins: all four springy, straight, and actually touching their pads.
One module completely dead (no homing) Pogo-pin contact on the 12V/G pads — clip the module off and on, look for a bent or stuck pin, check the bus-board chaining wires for that section.
Drum spins ~1½ turns, then gives up somewhere random Homing failed — the Hall sensor never saw the magnet. Sensor lead plugged in? Sensor seated close to the drum's path? Still nothing: the magnet is glued in upside-down — flip it.
Motor buzzes / twitches but won't turn Motor plug half-seated; or it's a 5 V 28BYJ-48 (the board needs the 12 V version); or the gear train is jammed — power off and turn the drum gently by hand.
Characters consistently off by whole flaps Flaps loaded into the drum shifted — reload with the blank at the home position (step 8c).
Characters slightly misaligned in the window Nudge the home offset with s (step 12).
Right characters, but drift as it runs Run calibrate (c); check the gears for debris or skipping.
Modules reset or misbehave when many move at once Undersized 12 V supply, or thin bus wiring dropping voltage along the row — use thicker wire for the 12V/G chain and feed power in at more than one point.
Upload fails in Arduino IDE Programmer must be SerialUPDI — 230400 baud; check the port, the Friend's 3V switch, and that the three wires match their labels. Use Upload Using Programmer, not Upload.
